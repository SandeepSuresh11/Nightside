Why it looks like that

Real curtains are shaped by the magnetic field, so the rays hang almost straight down no matter which way the band runs. Colour is layered by altitude: oxygen glows green around 100–150 km, and in a strong storm a red crown appears above it at 200–400 km. The pink fringe along the bottom edge is nitrogen, and it only shows up when the aurora is genuinely bright.

What Kp actually measures

Kp is a 0–9 index of global geomagnetic disturbance, averaged over three hours from magnetometers around the world. It tells you how far the auroral oval has pushed towards the equator, not whether the sky above you is clear.

Inside the oval — northern Norway, Iceland, interior Alaska, Yukon — aurora appears on quiet nights at Kp 1. From Seattle or Edinburgh you generally need Kp 6+. From Chicago or London, Kp 7–8.

Why Bz matters more than Kp for the next hour

Bz is the north–south component of the interplanetary magnetic field. When it turns negative, it reconnects with Earth's field and pours energy into the magnetosphere. Sustained Bz below −10 nT is the best short-term sign that something is about to happen.

Kp lags by up to three hours. Bz is measured about an hour upstream at L1, so it is your early warning.

How the aurora score works

Four factors, multiplied: aurora strength (forecast Kp against your geomagnetic latitude, blended with the OVATION grid), darkness (sun more than 12° below the horizon for full score), cloud, and moonlight while the moon is up. It is a heuristic, not a physical model.

Reading the Moon view

The disc shows the Moon as it appears from Earth right now — real phase, real terminator. Dark patches are the maria, drawn at their true selenographic coordinates. Drag it and you can turn past the limb onto the far side, which really is almost free of maria: the near side is about 31% mare, the far side about 2%.

The Moon keeps one face towards us, but not perfectly. Libration lets us see about 59% of the surface over a month. The view accounts for the optical libration in longitude and latitude.

By default the surface is drawn from real feature coordinates rather than photographed. For a photographic Moon, use Choose a lunar map from this phone at the bottom of this tab and pick an equirectangular lunar map — the phase, terminator and libration all still work, the surface is just the real one. It is stored on your device, not uploaded.

Reading the Sun view

Active regions come from NOAA's joint USAF/NOAA Solar Region Summary. Marker size follows the reported sunspot area, so a big blot really is a big group, and a group with many spots is drawn as a cluster rather than one dot. Numbers turn red for a Delta magnetic class — the ones that produce X-class flares.

The disc is drawn rather than photographed, and it runs continuously: granulation, a breathing corona, prominences on the limb, and the surface turning at the Sun's real rate of about 14° a day at the equator. Drag it to spin ahead and see what is about to come round the east limb.

What is real is the spots. Every group comes from NOAA's Solar Region Summary at its reported heliographic latitude, longitude and area, foreshortened towards the limb the way a real group is.

Region numbers turn red when the region carries a Delta magnetic class — those are the ones that produce X-class flares.

Reading the star view

Around 70 of the brightest stars, drawn at their catalogue positions, plus the planets computed from orbital elements. Positions are good to a few arcminutes — far better than you need to find something by eye.

Magnitude runs backwards: lower is brighter. Sirius is −1.5, the faintest star most people can see from a dark site is about +6, and from a city more like +3.

Tap any labelled star or planet and the view glides onto it, with a card showing distance, brightness and — for planets — the real phase and apparent size. The picture on that card is an illustration drawn from the real numbers, not a photograph.

Honest note on what is real here: the 76 named stars, the planets, the Milky Way band and the constellation lines are all computed from catalogue positions. The much fainter background stars are scenery, added so the sky does not look bare — they are not real positions, are never labelled, and cannot be tapped. Their density does rise towards the galactic plane, which is true of the real sky.

Where this data comes from
  • NOAA SWPC — Kp, OVATION, solar wind, solar regions, F10.7 flux, watches and warnings. US Government work, public domain.
  • Open-Meteo — hourly cloud cover and place search. Free, no key.
  • Esri / OpenStreetMap — map tiles.
  • Sun, Moon, planet and star positions are computed on your device from published orbital elements and catalogue coordinates. Nothing about you is sent anywhere.
