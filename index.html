What Kp actually measures

Kp is a 0–9 index of global geomagnetic disturbance, averaged over three hours from magnetometers around the world. It is a proxy, not a forecast: it tells you how far the auroral oval has pushed towards the equator, not whether the sky above you is clear.

Inside the oval — northern Norway, Iceland, interior Alaska, Yukon — aurora appears on quiet nights at Kp 1. From Seattle or Edinburgh you generally need Kp 6+. From Chicago or London, Kp 7–8.

Why Bz matters more than Kp for the next hour

Bz is the north–south component of the interplanetary magnetic field. When it turns negative (southward), it reconnects with Earth's field and pours energy into the magnetosphere. Sustained Bz below −10 nT is the single best short-term sign that something is about to happen.

Kp lags by up to three hours. Bz is measured about an hour upstream at the L1 point, so it is your early warning.

How this app scores your night

Four factors, multiplied together:

  • Aurora strength — forecast Kp compared against your geomagnetic latitude, plus the NOAA OVATION probability grid where it covers you.
  • Darkness — the sun must be more than 12° below the horizon for a full score; nothing counts above −6°.
  • Cloud — total cover from Open-Meteo, hour by hour.
  • Moon — illuminated fraction, but only while the moon is above your horizon.

It is a heuristic, not a physical model. Treat a low score as a reason to check the map, not a reason to stay in.

Finding a dark spot
  • Get a clear, unobstructed view of the northern horizon — southern if you're below the equator.
  • Drive away from town, then keep going. Fifteen minutes rarely does it; 45 usually does.
  • Water and open fields beat treelines. A low aurora on the horizon is the most commonly missed one.
  • Give your eyes 20 minutes to adapt, and keep your screen on the dimmest red setting you have.
Where this data comes from
  • NOAA SWPC — planetary Kp (1-minute and 3-day forecast), OVATION aurora model, real-time solar wind from DSCOVR/ACE, and the watch/warning feed. US Government work, public domain.
  • Open-Meteo — hourly cloud cover and place search. Free for non-commercial use, no key needed.
  • OpenStreetMap — map tiles, © OpenStreetMap contributors.
  • Sun and moon positions are computed on your device. Nothing about you is sent anywhere.
